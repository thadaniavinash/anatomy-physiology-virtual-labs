Synaptic transmission & initiating the impulse

A chemical message crosses the synapse and creates a graded potential (EPSP or IPSP) in the next neuron. If enough EPSPs add up at the axon hillock to reach threshold, an action potential starts.

    Key points
    1. Electrical → chemical → electrical. An action potential opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels; Ca²⁺ triggers release of neurotransmitter, which opens ligand-gated channels on the next neuron.
    2. EPSP vs IPSP. Excitatory transmitters (e.g., glutamate) let Na⁺ in and depolarize (EPSP). Inhibitory transmitters (e.g., GABA) let Cl⁻ in and hyperpolarize (IPSP).
    3. Graded, not all-or-none. Postsynaptic potentials vary in size and fade as they spread. One EPSP is usually too small to reach threshold.
    4. Summation. EPSPs add up over time (temporal) or from several synapses (spatial); IPSPs subtract. If the axon hillock reaches threshold (−55 mV), an action potential starts, which Part 2 follows down the axon.
    5. Ending the signal. Glutamate and GABA are removed mainly by reuptake into the terminal (and nearby glial cells) or diffuse away, so each signal is brief. At acetylcholine synapses, such as the neuromuscular junction, the enzyme acetylcholinesterase breaks the transmitter down instead.

    Timing is slowed and simplified for teaching. Only one synapse is drawn in close-up; the neuron diagram shows all three.

    Clinical connections. Myasthenia gravis: antibodies block acetylcholine receptors. Botulinum toxin blocks neurotransmitter release. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (e.g., neostigmine, donepezil) keep ACh in the cleft longer. SSRIs block serotonin reuptake. Benzodiazepines enhance GABA receptors, increasing IPSPs.

    Excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters

    Whether a neurotransmitter excites or inhibits depends on the receptor it binds: the same chemical can do both in different tissues. Each card lists the usual effect, where it is used, why it matters in nursing practice, and a first look at drugs that act on it in the brain and spinal cord (CNS).

    How the signal ends. The action of most neurotransmitters is ended by reuptake into the presynaptic neuron, removal by astrocytes (neuroglia), and/or diffusion away from the synapse. Acetylcholine is the main exception: the enzyme acetylcholinesterase breaks it down in the synaptic cleft.
