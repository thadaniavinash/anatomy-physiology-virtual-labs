Continuous conduction in an unmyelinated axon

An action potential is not a packet of sodium sliding down the axon. Each patch of membrane regenerates the signal: local current brings the next patch to threshold, its own Na⁺ channels open, and K⁺ efflux then restores the negative interior.

How to read this simulation

Regeneration, not a travelling packet of sodium. Na⁺ ions enter only where channels are open and then mix into the nearby axoplasm (the particles fade as they join it). The signal travels because positive charge spreading inside the axon (local current) brings the next patch of membrane to threshold, where it opens its own Na⁺ channels.

Every channel follows the same cycle. Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels go closed → open → inactivated → closed. They open within a fraction of a millisecond of depolarization and inactivate soon after, so Na⁺ entry peaks before the voltage peak. They recover from inactivation only once the membrane has repolarized. Voltage-gated K⁺ channels open more slowly (delayed), stay open through repolarization and close gradually during the afterhyperpolarization.

Why ion flow slows even with channels open. Ion movement depends on the open channels and the driving force: the difference between the membrane voltage and that ion's equilibrium potential (ENa ≈ +60 mV, EK ≈ −90 mV). Near the peak, the voltage approaches ENa, so Na⁺ influx weakens. During the afterhyperpolarization, the voltage is close to EK, so K⁺ efflux almost stops even though many K⁺ channels are still open.

Why the wave only moves forward. Local current spreads in both directions, but the membrane behind the wave is refractory: its Na⁺ channels are still inactivated. The absolute refractory period lasts until enough Na⁺ channels have recovered. During the relative refractory period, partly recovered Na⁺ channels and still-open K⁺ channels mean a stronger-than-normal stimulus is needed.

Tiny amounts of ions. Only a very small fraction of the ions near the membrane cross during one action potential; bulk concentrations barely change. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump (not drawn) restores the gradients over the long term — it is not what repolarizes the membrane. Resting leak channels, which set the resting potential, are also omitted for clarity.

Teaching simplifications. This is a prescribed waveform, not a Hodgkin–Huxley calculation. Illustrative values: rest −75 mV, threshold −55 mV, peak +30 mV, afterhyperpolarization −85 mV; conduction velocity 0.75 m/s (typical of thin unmyelinated C fibres, about 0.5–2 m/s). Channel spacing, particle numbers and arrows are schematic.

Clinical connection. Local anaesthetics such as lidocaine block voltage-gated Na⁺ channels. If a stretch of axon cannot open its Na⁺ channels, the action potential cannot be regenerated there and conduction stops — which is how a nerve block prevents pain signals from reaching the brain. Slow, unmyelinated C fibres carry dull, aching "second pain".
