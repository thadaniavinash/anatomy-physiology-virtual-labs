Red cell antigens and antibodies

Every red blood cell (RBC) carries molecules on its surface called antigens. Antibodies in plasma can recognize and bind antigens that the body sees as foreign. Blood typing identifies which antigens are present, so that a client receives blood their immune system will not attack.

Key terms

Antigen
A molecule (here, a sugar or protein on the RBC membrane) that can trigger an immune response.
Antibody
A Y-shaped immunoglobulin in plasma that binds a specific antigen. Each antibody has at least two binding sites.
Agglutination
Clumping of red cells when antibodies bind matching antigens on neighbouring cells.
Hemolysis
Destruction of red cells, for example when antibodies activate complement against them.

Many blood group systems

More than 40 blood group systems and over 300 red cell antigens are recognized. Most rarely cause problems. Two systems matter most in everyday practice: ABO, because its antibodies are already present without any exposure, and Rh (D), because the D antigen readily provokes antibody formation.

The ABO system

The A and B antigens are sugar chains built on a precursor called the H antigen. A person inherits one ABO gene from each parent. The A and B genes are codominant, and the O gene is recessive (it adds no sugar, so the H antigen is left unchanged).

From early infancy (around 3–6 months), everyone forms antibodies against whichever A or B antigen they lack, probably after exposure to similar sugars on gut bacteria. These naturally occurring anti-A and anti-B antibodies are mainly IgM. They activate complement strongly, so ABO-incompatible red cells are destroyed rapidly inside blood vessels.

The Rh (D) system

“Rh positive” means the D antigen is present on the red cells, and “Rh negative” means it is absent. In Canada about 85% of people are Rh positive.

  • Anti-D is not naturally occurring. An Rh-negative person forms it only after exposure to Rh-positive red cells, through transfusion or pregnancy.
  • Anti-D is IgG, so it can cross the placenta. In a later pregnancy it can destroy the red cells of an Rh-positive fetus: hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN).
  • Rh immune globulin (RhIG) prevents this. It is given to Rh-negative pregnant clients (routinely around 28 weeks, and after the birth of an Rh-positive baby or other sensitizing events) so they do not form anti-D.

How the slide test works

Each slide in the simulation contains a reagent antibody: anti-A, anti-B or anti-Rh (anti-D). If the client’s red cells carry the matching antigen, the antibodies link the cells together and visible clumps form. If not, the blood stays smooth and evenly red.

Agglutination with a reagent antibody tells you the antigen is present. For example, clumping with anti-B and anti-Rh but not anti-A means the cells carry B and D antigens only: type B+. Try the simulation →

Red cell compatibility

Donor red cells must not carry any antigen the recipient lacks, or the recipient’s antibodies will attack them.

O− : universal red cell donor
No A, B or D antigens, so it is used in emergencies before the client’s type is known.
AB+ : universal red cell recipient
Has A, B and D antigens and no anti-A or anti-B, so it can receive red cells of any type.

Blood transfusion: nursing considerations

Most serious transfusion reactions are preventable, and the most dangerous, ABO incompatibility, is usually caused by an identification error at sample collection or at the bedside. The nurse is the final safety check before blood reaches the client.

This section focuses on red blood cells. Always follow your agency’s transfusion policy, which may differ in details such as time limits and monitoring frequency.

Before Preparing the client

  • Verify the order: product, number of units, rate and any special requirements (e.g., irradiated products).
  • Informed consent is obtained and documented. Ask about previous transfusions and reactions, and about beliefs that affect accepting blood (e.g., Jehovah’s Witnesses).
  • Pre-transfusion sample: a current group and screen or crossmatch, with the sample labelled at the bedside immediately after collection.
  • Baseline assessment within 30 minutes before starting: temperature, pulse, respirations, blood pressure, SpO₂, lung sounds and fluid status. Note clients at higher risk of overload (older adults, heart or kidney failure).
  • IV access: commonly 20–22 G for routine adult transfusion and 16–18 G for rapid transfusion. Smaller catheters can be used for fragile veins and children.
  • Equipment: a blood administration set with a 170–260 µm filter, primed with 0.9% sodium chloride only. Never add medications to blood or run it with dextrose or other solutions.
  • Pick up one unit at a time and start promptly. If it cannot be started within the agency’s time limit, return it to the blood bank.
  • Bedside check with a second qualified person: match two client identifiers on the wristband, requisition and unit tag; check the unit number, ABO/Rh, product and expiry; inspect the bag for clots, discolouration or leaks.

During Starting and monitoring

  • Start slowly (e.g., about 50 mL/h) for the first 15 minutes and stay with the client. Severe reactions usually appear early, after only a small volume. Repeat for each unit.
  • Vital signs after the first 15 minutes, then at least hourly (or per policy), and at the end of the unit.
  • If there are no concerns, increase to the ordered rate. A unit of red cells usually runs over 1.5–2 hours, more slowly for clients at risk of overload.
  • Complete within 4 hours of leaving controlled storage.
  • Teach the client to report chills, itching, rash, shortness of breath, back pain or feeling unwell right away.

After Follow-up

  • Vital signs at completion and afterwards per policy (e.g., within an hour for inpatients).
  • Document the product, unit number, volume, start and stop times, vital signs and the client’s response.
  • Evaluate effect (e.g., symptoms, post-transfusion hemoglobin as ordered).
  • Teach the client to report fever, jaundice, dark urine or breathlessness in the following days, since delayed reactions can occur.

If you suspect a reaction

  1. Stop the transfusion immediately.
  2. Keep the IV open with 0.9% sodium chloride through new tubing. Do not flush the blood line.
  3. Assess the client: airway, breathing, circulation and vital signs. Stay with the client.
  4. Recheck identification: client wristband against the unit tag and paperwork, at the bedside.
  5. Notify the prescriber and the blood bank (transfusion service).
  6. Treat as ordered: for example oxygen, an antipyretic, an antihistamine, epinephrine for anaphylaxis, or a diuretic and upright positioning for TACO.
  7. Return the bag and tubing to the blood bank and collect blood and urine samples per protocol.
  8. Document and report the reaction through the transfusion service.
For isolated mild hives with stable vital signs, the prescriber may order an antihistamine and allow the same unit to be restarted slowly. Never restart a transfusion after fever, hypotension or breathing difficulty without a full investigation.

Plasma transfusion

Plasma is the liquid part of blood, about 55% of its volume. It is mostly water and carries proteins (albumin, immunoglobulins and clotting factors), electrolytes, nutrients and wastes. Plasma is transfused mainly to replace clotting factors.

Plasma products in Canada

  • Frozen plasma (FP) and apheresis fresh frozen plasma (AFP): contain all the clotting factors.
  • Solvent/detergent-treated plasma (Octaplasma): pooled plasma treated to inactivate many viruses.
  • Cryoprecipitate: a concentrated source of fibrinogen (also factor VIII, von Willebrand factor and factor XIII).
  • Plasma protein products such as albumin, IVIG and prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) are manufactured from plasma and are handled differently.
When plasma is indicated
  • Bleeding, or an invasive procedure, when several clotting factors are deficient (e.g., liver disease, DIC)
  • Massive hemorrhage with clotting abnormalities
  • Therapeutic plasma exchange (e.g., TTP)
When plasma is not indicated
  • Volume replacement
  • A mildly raised INR (below 1.8) before a procedure in a client who is not bleeding
  • Warfarin reversal: use PCC with vitamin K instead
  • When a specific factor concentrate is available

Plasma compatibility: the rule is reversed

With red cells, we ask: which antigens are on the donor cells? With plasma, we ask: which antibodies are in the donor plasma? Donor plasma must not contain antibodies against the recipient’s own red cell antigens.

AB : universal plasma donor
AB plasma has neither anti-A nor anti-B, so any client can receive it.
O : universal plasma recipient
Group O red cells have no A or B antigens, so a group O client can receive plasma of any ABO group.
Rh is not a factor in plasma transfusion, and plasma is not crossmatched. Only ABO compatibility is needed, so the client’s ABO/Rh group must be known.

Nursing considerations

  • Plan ahead: plasma is stored frozen. Thawing takes at least 20 minutes (30 minutes for solvent/detergent plasma). Thawed plasma cannot be refrozen and is kept refrigerated in the blood bank for up to 5 days.
  • Dose: usually 10–15 mL/kg, as ordered, which often means several units. Watch the total fluid volume.
  • Same safety checks as red cells: consent, two-person bedside identification, blood administration set with filter, 0.9% sodium chloride only, stay with the client for the first 15 minutes, and complete within 4 hours.
  • Monitor closely for: TACO (large volumes), TRALI (plasma-rich products carry the highest risk) and allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis.
  • Evaluate the effect: bleeding and repeat coagulation tests (INR, aPTT, fibrinogen) as ordered.
